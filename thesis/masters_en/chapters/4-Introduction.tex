\section{Motivation}
Quantum computing is moving from textbook algorithms toward concrete uses in signal and image processing~\cite{Ruan2021OpportunitiesChallenges,Wang2022ReviewQIP,Farooq2025SystematicReview}, thanks to recent technological advances. When it comes to quantum image processing, it wants to encode an image in the state of a small qubit register, then run an operation such as filtering, segmentation, or edge detection directly onto that state~\cite{Le2011FRQI,Yao2017QHED}. To achieve this, some bottlenecks need to be solved first. In the case of Noisy Intermediate-Scale Quantum (NISQ) devices, they have few qubits and short coherence times, and their gate and readout errors are far from negligible. As a result, circuit depth and two-qubit gate count decide whether a pipeline runs at all, long before any asymptotic speedup could matter~\cite{Preskill2018NISQ,Geng2022HybridNISQEdge,Geng2023ImprovedFRQI}.

This thesis studies one concrete pipeline end to end: the Flexible Representation of Quantum Images (FRQI)~\cite{Le2011FRQI}, followed by the Quantum Hadamard Edge Detector (QHED)~\cite{Yao2017QHED,QiskitTextbookQuantumEdgeDetection}. FRQI is a natural test case, because its state preparation is dominated by MCX (Multi-Controlled NOT) gates, which are exactly the operations that inflate depth and CX (Controlled-NOT) count on NISQ devices.

\section{Problem and research question}
FRQI stores each grayscale intensity in a rotation angle on one \emph{color} qubit, addressed by a \emph{position} register of $m = 2\log_2 N$ qubits for an $N\times N$ image~\cite{Le2011FRQI,Iliyasu2013FRQIroadmap}. Preparing the state means looping over pixel addresses and applying a multi-controlled rotation for each one. The cost of that loop depends entirely on how the multi-controlled gates are synthesized, and modern synthesis work shows that ancilla-assisted decompositions can cut CX depth substantially~\cite{Vale2023MCSU2,Azevedo2024LowDepthMCG,Arsoski2024MCXQFT,Zindorf2024EfficientMCG}.

The research questions this thesis wants to answer are: \textbf{when the only change is the multi-controlled synthesis used to prepare the same FRQI state, how do transpiled resources and noise robustness change? Does any resource saving carry through to the edge-detection result?} To keep the comparison as precise as possible, QHED and its classical Sobel reference will remain the same; only the preparation circuit and its noise channel shall vary. Because readout error biases are a big deal in the quantum domain and on  future, real devices~\cite{Mu2025GuidedImageFiltering}, a small readout-mitigation slice will be added on the color qubit as well.

Prior quantum edge-detection work usually assumes ideal preparation or studies NEQR-based Sobel variants~\cite{Zhang2013NEQR,Zhang2015QSobel,Fan2019SobelNEQR,Chetia2021ImprovedSobelNEQR}. Few studies compare naive against ancilla-assisted FRQI preparation on identical noise grids while keeping the downstream stage fixed.

\section{Contributions}
This thesis brings the following contributions:
\begin{enumerate}
  \item A \textbf{comparison} between naive (\texttt{no-ancilla}) and v-chain (ancilla-assisted) multi-controlled FRQI state preparation, in which the metrics related to transpiled qubit count, depth, and CX for $4\times 4$, $8\times 8$, and $16\times 16$ images are reported.
  \item A \textbf{study related to variable noise} in which FRQI states are being reconstructed under depolarizing noise, followed by the QHED block. In the end, the metrics PSNR, SSIM, and state fidelity are provided.
  \item A \textbf{proof of concept regarding readout-mitigation} in which a $2\times 2$ confusion matrix is being estimated for the color qubit. This matrix is then inverted, and it is being compared between its raw version and its mitigated form for $p_1$ across random seeds on a $4\times 4$ slice.
  \item \textbf{ A complete pipeline for Quantum Image Processing on NISQ devices:} FRQI followed by QHED and at the end, which reports a variety of metrics.
\end{enumerate}
All of them  are backed by the repository.\footnote{\href{https://github.com/AlexandruAlex10/qip-frqi-qhed-mcx-ancilla-and-calibration-matrix-inversion}{GitHub repository}}

\section{Scope and limitations}
All experiments were done in a simulated environment, without access to real IBM hardware. The test images were limited to small sizes of $4\times 4$, $8\times 8$, and $16\times 16$, because, in the case of FRQI v-chain state preparation for the $16\times 16$ image, the density matrix generation required a large amount of RAM, which exceeded the available resources on the machine that was used for running the experiments. As a result, this experiment is often skipped in the thesis.
